# Product Brief

## Working title

**where-is-school**

The title is intentionally lowercase and slightly awkward. Preserve this spelling in repository metadata and public-facing materials unless the owner approves a change.

## One-sentence pitch

An adult wakes at a school desk as a student again, then tries to get through an ordinary school day and find a way out of a school whose classrooms, memories, and geography refuse to agree.

## Genre and format

- Painterly 2.5D narrative RPG / exploration adventure.
- Single-player, story-led, offline-first.
- Windows PC first; Steam distribution is a long-term target.
- Exploration, environmental observation, and literary dialogue are the primary verbs.
- No combat in the current scope.

## Player fantasy

The player is not a hero saving a world. They are an adult who has inexplicably become a student again. They wake in class without a reliable account of how they got there, discover that other students seem to know their history, and try to evade an expected lesson, find a route, or simply leave.

The immediate concerns are embarrassingly ordinary: not having the right textbook, being questioned, being judged, forgetting a timetable, being treated as a student, and wanting to get out. Those concerns gradually lead into spaces that should not connect and encounters that cannot be classified.

## Design pillars

### 1. Unstable but legible spaces

School is not a building with a fixed address; it is a condition that can attach itself to classrooms, residential compounds, offices, parking lots, ports, and other ordinary places. Different versions of the school can contradict one another. The player should be disoriented in fiction but never confused about basic controls or immediate objectives.

### 2. Conversation as action

Dialogue is not decorative lore. Ordinary chatter, rumors, omissions, competing recollections, and interior voices reveal information and change how the player interprets people and places. Characters have their own agendas and should not function as quest terminals.

### 3. Everyday objects carry the surrealism

A wooden set square, textbooks, a teacher's desk, chalk dust, a damp floor, a rubbish bin, laundry lines, tires, containers, and phone maps recur across scenes. Objects should retain their material specificity; their meaning can shift without an explanatory caption.

### 4. Adult identity inside a student role

The protagonist is an adult occupying a student position again. The central tension is not simply fear of school, but resistance to being classified, evaluated, corrected, and made to perform the role of a student. The protagonist may be afraid of authority and simultaneously imagine ridiculous ways to frustrate it.

### 5. Observation without guaranteed classification

The game invites close inspection of people, objects, and living things. Naming or categorizing something does not guarantee understanding. Biological language and school systems of evaluation may echo one another, but the game should not explain the analogy for the player.

### 6. Restrained visual authorship

The game should feel authored and materially coherent. A small number of excellent spaces is preferable to a large number of generic environments.

## Core gameplay loop

1. Receive or infer a concrete short-term objective (find a room, person, stairway, gate, textbook, or clue).
2. Traverse an unstable but navigable space.
3. Inspect objects and environmental details.
4. Talk to people and hear competing accounts and interior voices.
5. Discover a contradiction, memory fragment, or new route condition.
6. Change the world state or reinterpret a previous place.
7. Enter the next space with a new question.

## Opening experience

The opening chapter is **Biology Class**. The protagonist wakes at a classroom desk with a headache and unstable memory. A familiar-but-unremembered classmate, Q, talks about the final term, the protagonist's previous blank biology exam, and the absent biology teacher B. The protagonist cannot find their biology textbook and imagines an elaborate confrontation with B. When the bell rings, B does not appear: an inexperienced substitute, N, arrives instead and eventually permits students to leave for free activity.

The protagonist exits the classroom, then the school grounds, moving through ordinary urban and residential spaces. A strange green animal becomes the focus of increasingly careful but inconclusive observation. The opening should preserve the distinction between what the protagonist remembers, what Q claims, what the protagonist imagines, and what actually happens.

## Core systems (eventual scope)

- Character movement and a readable 2.5D camera.
- Contextual interaction and inspectable objects.
- Dialogue with long-form text, choices, portraits, and story flags.
- Five provisional inner voices: 路感, 学籍, 羞耻, 旁听, and 倦怠.
- Lightweight skill/check system where failure can reveal alternate information rather than simply blocking progress.
- Journal / route notebook whose records can become unreliable.
- Scene transitions and stateful environmental changes.
- Versioned save/load, accessibility, and input settings.

## Scope guardrails

**First vertical slice:** one compact classroom opening, Q as the first conversational NPC, several inspectable classroom objects (including the schoolbag/textbook search and the wooden set square as a narrative object), one meaningful conversation with distinct choices, and a short route from the classroom toward the school grounds. A later slice can test the transition from the school to the residential streets and the green animal encounter.

**Not in the first slice:** all chapters, combat, inventory-heavy RPG systems, procedural generation, open world, voice acting, cinematic facial animation, online features, localization pipeline, final production art for every character, or a complete implementation of every memory branch.

## Audience and positioning

For players who enjoy literary narrative games, atmospheric exploration, surreal architecture, close environmental observation, and dialogue-driven experiences. The game should be understandable without knowing any literary or game references.

## First-slice success criteria

A first-time player can:
- understand movement and interaction without a tutorial wall;
- recognize the classroom and its objects as a coherent place;
- understand Q's immediate conversational role without treating him as an exposition terminal;
- make choices that express different attitudes toward memory and the expected biology lesson;
- experience the difference between an imagined confrontation and B's actual absence;
- finish with a clear next action or question.

The slice duration should be set after a playable build exists; do not treat the earlier 10–20 minute estimate as a fixed requirement.
