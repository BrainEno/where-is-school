# Narrative Bible

## Core premise

The protagonist is an adult who wakes at a school desk and finds themself occupying the position of a student again. They cannot reliably remember why they are there, how they returned, or what happened in the preceding term. The school treats their student status as ordinary, while their own memory resists it.

The school has no stable address. Its different versions connect through contradictory corridors, staircases, classrooms, residential compounds, yards, and eventually the elliptical building. These versions are not confirmed parallel universes, psychological projections, or technological simulations. Their cause remains unknown.

The protagonist's return does not require a final explanation. The central experience is trying to move through a place that appears to know the protagonist better than the protagonist knows themself.

## Central question

**Where is the school—and what would count as leaving it?**

The game should not reduce this question to a single hidden explanation. Its emotional truth matters more than a final metaphysical answer.

## Themes

- Memory as unstable reconstruction: some details are excessively vivid while essential facts and routes disappear.
- Institutional roles that persist through repetition, performance, and ordinary acts of authority.
- Adulthood interrupted by a return to the student position.
- Shame, evaluation, punishment, evasion, and the desire to escape being classified.
- Observation and classification: the difference between naming a living thing and understanding it.
- The gap between an imagined event and what actually happens.
- Everyday objects that acquire disturbing significance without becoming explained symbols.
- Places that contain traces of people but do not reliably remember them.

## Rules of the world

1. **Geography is conditional.** The same staircase, door, classroom, or yard can connect to different school versions at different moments.
2. **The elliptical building connects school versions.** It is not simply a portal between unrelated geographic locations. It is a later-discovered spatial structure; do not introduce it in the opening.
3. **The cause of spatial change is unknown.** Do not settle whether it comes from consciousness, memory, an external force, or something else.
4. **Time is administrative as well as chronological.** Terms, lessons, attendance, and examinations can be pending, resumed, or already over according to whoever is speaking.
5. **Identity is contextual.** The protagonist is an adult, yet other people treat them as a student. Neither status is fully stable.
6. **The protagonist's memory contains gaps and excess.** A vivid recollection is not proof that the remembered event happened exactly that way.
7. **Imagined scenes are not objective flashbacks.** Distinguish what the protagonist remembers, what another person claims, what the protagonist imagines, and what is observed in the present.
8. **The world does not explain its own symbolism.** Avoid an omniscient character who translates every strange event into a definitive allegory.
9. **Anomalies may have learnable limits, not complete explanations.** Observation can help the player anticipate some consequences without revealing the underlying cause.

## Protagonist

An adult caught in the role of a repeat student. They are observant, defensive, evasive, curious, tired, and capable of sudden absurd or provocative fantasies. They may anticipate humiliation while also imagining ways to ridicule, frustrate, or outlast the person who might humiliate them.

Do not reduce this contradiction to a diagnosis, a simple childhood-trauma explanation, or a conventional rebellion arc. The protagonist may resist the student role before understanding why it has returned. They can be both frightened by authority and unwilling to grant it the satisfaction of seeing that fear.

Do not diagnose the protagonist or present an inferred medical condition as the explanation for the world.

## Opening chapter: Biology Class

The opening begins inside a classroom, not with the protagonist searching for the school entrance. The protagonist wakes at a desk with a severe headache and unstable memory. The exact status of the scene—as present, memory, or something between them—should not be explained immediately.

A classmate, Q, speaks to the protagonist. Q seems familiar with them and talks with intrusive ease, but the protagonist does not remember him. He is an ordinary, talkative, somewhat annoying classmate rather than a mysterious guide. His low-value chatter can casually reveal consequential details without announcing them as clues.

Q says this is the final term, recalls that the protagonist handed in a blank biology exam the previous term, and warns that the biology teacher B may question or punish them. The protagonist cannot remember biology lessons clearly and may remember incompatible versions of B. The first memory choice offers several directions of recollection (A/B/C) or the option to defer active recollection (D). These are entry attitudes and scene-order influences, not four permanently exclusive campaigns. Their specific content remains to be authored; do not assume a fixed mapping to exams, teachers, or escape.

### B: the absent biology teacher

B is a woman, an elderly and severe biology teacher in the protagonist's recollection. She is associated with a large wooden set square that originally belongs to mathematics, has three pointed corners, is worn and cracked, and is loosely repaired with tape. She grips, adjusts, and taps it. It is at once an imprecise classroom object, a fragile thing, a potential weapon, and a visible sign of authority. It can be used to strike a desk or a student's palm.

B's characterization has two intertwined registers:

- **Institutional authority:** sternness, inspection, correction, punishment, the raised platform, the teacher's desk, the textbook, and the right to decide what counts as a correct answer.
- **Petty, ordinary personhood:** pride, suspicion, concern for face and classroom control, small resentments, and self-interested judgments. These qualities should appear as human pettiness, not as a cartoon villain reveal.

The protagonist's account of B is not a neutral character dossier. It contains memory, fear, hostility, fantasy, and speculation about what B might think. Q's account, the protagonist's recollection, and B's eventual observable behavior should never fit together perfectly.

B's authority is partly a role she performs and sustains. The inspiration is the theatrical relationship between role, audience, and authority—not a secret identity, a second personality, or a simple fraud. Do not decide prematurely whether B consciously knows she is performing or has fully internalized the teacher role.

B is absent from the opening biology lesson. Her absence should have weight: Q's warnings and the protagonist's elaborate imagined confrontation occupy the scene, but the expected confrontation does not happen. Do not bring B onstage immediately to validate or disprove the stories about her.

### Q: the familiar stranger

Q is a talkative, overweight, self-assured classmate who behaves as if he and the protagonist have known each other for a long time. The protagonist cannot remember him. Q is not a supernatural messenger, a deliberate liar, or a quest-information dispenser. He chats, speculates, repeats school gossip, and casually supplies details about the protagonist's past. His familiarity can be intrusive and irritating. Important information should emerge incidentally from his ordinary speech.

### N: the substitute teacher

N is a young, inexperienced substitute who appears because B is ill and may be absent for an extended period. N is short enough to be partly obscured by the teacher's desk, struggles to command the room, and eventually allows students to study or leave for free activity. Do not make N the simple embodiment of truth or kindness in contrast to B's falseness. N is another person occupying the teacher role, with different competence and authority.

### The opening's dramatic structure

1. The protagonist wakes at a desk; Q starts talking.
2. Q describes the final term, the previous blank biology exam, and B's expected questioning.
3. The protagonist searches their bag for a biology textbook and finds other school materials but not the biology book.
4. The absence of the book intensifies anticipation. The protagonist imagines increasingly elaborate ways to evade, provoke, or humiliate B. These fantasies are subjective and may become physically absurd; do not present them as events that objectively occurred.
5. The bell rings. B does not arrive. N substitutes for her, awkwardly permits self-study and then free activity.
6. The protagonist leaves the classroom and moves through the school with a sudden, almost performative sense of freedom.
7. The protagonist leaves the school grounds. Ordinary streets, traffic, a residential compound, and children at play lead away from the classroom.
8. A strange green animal is glimpsed and then observed more closely. The protagonist struggles to classify it. The animal and the act of looking at it extend the biology chapter's concerns with life, naming, damage, and the limits of observation. The supplied source passage ends while the animal is moving; do not invent its eventual fate as established canon.

The chapter may initially be presented as the game's present, without a clear label announcing that it is a memory. The player should gradually recognize that the protagonist has left one school space without necessarily leaving the school condition.

## Memory choices: A / B / C / D

A, B, and C are active attempts by the protagonist to recollect or explain their situation. D is a refusal to force recollection: the player continues with immediate school life and allows people and places to bring memories forward. D is not simply a fourth equal branch.

The exact subject matter of A, B, and C is undecided. Do not assign them definitive topics without approval. An initial choice may affect which memory path is entered first, the order of scenes, and which evidence is encountered early; it need not permanently lock away other material. Keep memory claims distinct from independently observed evidence.

## Recurring motifs and objects

### The wooden set square

The set square is B's signature object and a central visual motif. Preserve its contradictions: borrowed from mathematics, used in biology; geometric but unstable in the hand; damaged yet threatening; taped but not safely neutralized; a classroom tool that can become an instrument of punishment. Its appearances elsewhere may vary in condition or context, but recurrence should not be explained as a supernatural tracking device.

### The green animal

The green, damaged-looking animal outside the school evokes classification failure. The protagonist is uncertain whether it is a bird, poultry, or something else, and imagines how its coloring and damaged feathers came about. Its biological identity should not be immediately resolved for the player. The scene should encourage close observation without turning the animal into a puzzle with a neat taxonomic answer.

### Other ordinary objects

Mops, wet floors, iron doors, laundry lines, tires, desks, exam sheets, fluorescent lights, phone maps, rubbish bins, containers, trucks, and temporary planks recur across chapters. Their recurrence builds meaning through context, not explanatory symbolism.

## Later spaces and scene sequence (provisional)

The following spaces remain part of the broader project, but their order is flexible and must be reconciled with the opening chapter above:

- Residential compounds and school entrances hidden among ordinary buildings.
- Classrooms with missing windows, unfinished walls, or impossible exteriors.
- Staircases whose landings, heights, and destinations change; routes obstructed by tires, rugs, discarded objects, water, or oversized plants.
- Laboratories, toilets, gyms, and corridors where institutional routines continue in altered forms.
- Container yards with red and blue containers, truck tires, temporary planks, freight vehicles, and the sea.
- Multiple versions of the school whose rooms, records, and inhabitants contradict one another.
- The elliptical building, discovered later, connecting versions of the school through unfinished and abandoned spatial mechanisms.
- An examination space where the deadline may be near, indefinite, postponed, or already passed.

Do not force every location into a single linear itinerary at this stage.

## Dialogue principles

- Prefer specific observations, contradictions, evasions, awkward pauses, and low-value chatter over abstract speeches.
- Let ordinary bureaucratic language become strange through context.
- Long dialogue must be broken by player observation, choices, movement, and silence.
- Characters should have independent agendas; they are not merely exposition dispensers.
- Let characters remember the same event differently without immediately identifying one account as the correct one.
- Distinguish remembered events, present observations, rumors, and imagined scenes in both writing and implementation.
- A choice should change tone, knowledge, relationship, access, or the player's interpretation. Avoid fake choices that collapse immediately to the same response without a reason.
- Keep the player's immediate objective legible even when the larger story is not.
